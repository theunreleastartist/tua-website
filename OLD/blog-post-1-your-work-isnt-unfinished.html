Inner Work

Your work isn't unfinished.
You are.

The real reason music creatives can't finish their work has nothing to do with the work. And until you understand that, nothing changes.

D
Daniel J. Ryder
May 2026 · 7 min read

There is a folder on your laptop. Or a notes app. Or a voice memo archive. Full of things that are almost done. Almost ready. Almost there.

You know the one. You've been adding to it for years. And every time you open it you feel some combination of guilt, frustration, and the vague sense that you're failing at something, though you couldn't say exactly what.

I want to talk about that folder. Not about how to empty it. But about what it actually means that it exists.

The story we tell ourselves

The most common explanation music creatives give for not finishing their work is time. Not enough of it. Life is full. There are jobs and families and responsibilities and by the time everything else is handled there's nothing left for the music.

This is true. And it's also not the real reason.

Because you've found time before. Late nights, early mornings, stolen hours. You've sat down with the work more times than you can count. And still the folder stays full. Still the release doesn't happen. Still the thing you've been working toward stays just out of reach.

Time isn't the problem. Something else is.

What's actually happening

Here's what I've observed, in my own life as an artist and music creative, in the work I do with music creatives, and across years of working with professionals, executives, and college students in other industries too. This pattern shows up everywhere. The unfinished business proposal. The thesis that's been almost done for two years. The career pivot that keeps getting delayed. The form it takes changes. What sits underneath it doesn't. When the work stays unfinished, it is almost never about the work itself.

The work isn't done because finishing it would require something from you that you're not yet ready to give. Not more hours. Not better equipment. Not a clearer strategy.

It would require you to be seen.

And being seen means being judged. And being judged means finding out whether the thing you've been carrying inside you, the vision, the identity, the belief that you have something real to say, is actually true.

"The unfinished work is safe. It can still be everything you hoped it would be. The finished work has to face reality."

Perfectionism is not about quality. It's about protection. If it's never done, it can never disappoint you. It can never disappoint the people you care about. It can never be proof that you weren't as good as you thought.

The folder isn't a failure of discipline. It's a very effective defense mechanism.

The version of you that's unfinished

There's a line that came to me after a session once, one of those thoughts that arrives fully formed and you know immediately it's true.

Your work isn't unfinished. You are.

The music isn't stuck. You are stuck. And the music is just the place where that stuckness shows up most clearly because it's the place that matters most to you.

The unfinished songs, the unreleased beats, the album that's been almost ready for two years, the business that hasn't launched, the career move that keeps getting pushed back, these aren't the problem. They're the symptom. The problem is the version of yourself that hasn't come out yet. The one that believes what they make is worth putting into the world. The one that can sit with the vulnerability of being seen and keep going anyway.

I've seen this in music creatives. I've seen it in executives leading high-level programs who keep the big idea in a drawer. I've seen it in college students who know exactly what they want to build but find every reason not to start. The environment changes. The industry changes. The internal experience is almost identical. This is a human pattern, not a music one.

That version of you is the work. Not the music.

What this means practically

I'm not saying the music doesn't matter. It does. Deeply. But if you try to solve a self problem with a strategy solution you will keep ending up in the same place.

More time won't fix it. A better DAW won't fix it. A release strategy won't fix it. A productivity system won't fix it.

What fixes it is going underneath. Asking the questions that are harder to ask than "what do I need to do next?"

What am I actually afraid of here? Not the vague general fear, the specific one. The one that shows up at 2am when the session is open and you can't make yourself start.

What would it mean about me if I put this out and nobody cared? What would it mean if they did care? What am I protecting by keeping this in the folder?

These aren't comfortable questions. They're also the only ones that lead anywhere real.

The thing nobody tells you

Finishing the work doesn't get easier until you do the inner work first. That's the part nobody talks about, not in music coaching, not in executive coaching, not in any space where people are trying to help other people move. Everyone wants to talk about the strategy, the release plan, the marketing, the roadmap. And those things matter. I work on them too.

But you can have the perfect release plan and still find yourself at 11pm staring at the session unable to hit export. Because the outer plan doesn't touch what's actually stopping you.

The inner work and the outer plan. Both. Not one without the other.

That's the whole thing. That's what I built The Unreleased Artist around. Because I was that person for a long time, the person with the full folder and the sound logic for why it hadn't happened yet. As an artist and music creative for over twenty years. As someone who has worked with professionals and executives and students across industries and watched the same pattern show up in all of them. And I know from the inside what it takes to move through it.

It's not a strategy. It's not a system. It's understanding what's actually in the way, which is almost never what it looks like on the surface, and then building the real plan to move through it.

The folder on your laptop is not evidence that you're failing. It's evidence that you care deeply about something and you haven't yet found the support you needed to get it out.

That support exists. And it starts with being honest about what's actually happening, not the surface story, but the real one.

You're not behind. You're not broken. You're just not finished yet.

Neither is the work.
