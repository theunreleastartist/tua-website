The Solo Creative

The loneliness nobody
talks about in music.

There is a specific kind of alone that comes with building something in music by yourself. It doesn't have a name. But you know exactly what it feels like.

D
Daniel J. Ryder
May 2026 · 8 min read

It's not the loneliness of having nobody around. You might have people around. Family, friends, maybe even a creative community of some kind. It's a different loneliness. More specific. Harder to name.

It's the loneliness of being the only person in the room who fully understands what you're building and what it costs to build it.

It's finishing something at midnight, something that actually moved you, something you're genuinely proud of, and having nobody to call. Not because there's nobody in your life. But because nobody in your life would quite understand why this one moment mattered as much as it did.

It's carrying the vision alone. The doubt alone. The decisions alone. The 2am questioning alone. The gap between what you hear in your head and what you can currently produce alone. All of it, alone.

That's the loneliness I want to talk about. Because I've never heard anyone name it directly. And unnamed things have a way of becoming heavier than they need to be.

The people who love you can't always help

The people in your life who love you want to support you. They say the right things. They ask how it's going. They tell you you're talented. And they mean all of it.

But there's a gap between support and understanding. And that gap is where the loneliness lives.

When you tell someone who doesn't live in this world that you've been working on the same project for six months, they look at you with a kind of gentle confusion. Take making music as an example. They don't understand that finishing it isn't the point. That the song, the beat, the track, whatever form it takes, has become something else entirely, a conversation between you and some part of yourself that you're still figuring out. They just think you should be done by now. But this isn't only about music. It's about every creative project a music creative pours themselves into and can't explain to someone on the outside.

When you tell them about the fear of releasing something they hear: just put it out, what's the worst that could happen. They're trying to help. But they don't know what it feels like to have made something that came from somewhere real inside you and to have to offer it to people who might not care, or worse, might care in completely the wrong way.

They can't know. Because they haven't been there.

The weight of doing every role

There's another dimension to this that doesn't get talked about enough. When you're doing this as a solo creative, whether you're a producer, a singer, a songwriter, an engineer building a studio, a music entrepreneur launching something from scratch, you're doing every role.

You're the artist and the critic. The creator and the editor. The visionary and the project manager. The one who believes in the work and the one who tears it apart at 3am.

There's nobody to share that weight with. Nobody to say "I think it's ready" when you can't trust your own judgment anymore because you've been too close to it for too long. Nobody to hold the vision when you temporarily lose sight of it. Nobody to tell you to stop overthinking the hi-hat and just commit.

"You're not just the artist. You're the entire team. And the entire team is exhausted."

This isn't a complaint. A lot of us chose this. The solo creative path has real advantages, full control, full ownership, the work shaped entirely by your own vision. But the cost of that control is that you carry everything. And carrying everything alone, for long enough, starts to cost you in ways you might not immediately recognise.

What it does over time

The weight of doing it alone doesn't usually announce itself dramatically. It accumulates quietly.

You notice you're less excited about opening the session than you used to be. The thing that used to feel electric feels like a task. You still do it but it costs more than it used to and gives back less.

You notice you're harder on the work than you were before. The internal critic has gotten louder. The gap between what you're making and what you think you should be making feels wider. Not because your work has gotten worse, often it's gotten significantly better, but because the standard you're holding yourself to has become disconnected from any external reality check.

You notice you've stopped talking about what you're working on. Not because you don't care, but because you've explained it too many times to people who couldn't quite meet you there, and the explaining itself has become another form of the loneliness.

None of this means something is wrong with you. It means you've been doing something genuinely hard without the support it deserves.

What actually helps

I've thought about this a lot. As an artist and music creative myself, I've lived this from the inside. I've been the person finishing something at midnight with nobody to call. I've carried the vision alone, made every decision alone, questioned everything alone. And in the work I do with music creatives who are in exactly this place, I see it reflected back every time.

What helps is not more inspiration. It's not a motivational video or a productivity system or a pep talk. Those things give you a temporary lift and then you're back in the same place, slightly more aware of the gap between where you are and where you wanted to be.

What helps is being understood. Fully. By someone who has been in this specific territory and who can hold your vision alongside you without flinching at its complexity or its cost.

What helps is having somewhere to put the weight down. Not permanently, the work requires the weight in some sense, it's what makes it real. But temporarily. A conversation where you don't have to explain why this matters because the person you're talking to already knows.

What helps is accountability that comes from someone who actually understands what they're holding you accountable to. Not generic accountability. Specific, informed accountability from someone who has sat in the same room, figuratively speaking, and knows what it takes to get out of it.

Why I built this

I'm not a person who talks about himself easily. But this part matters for what The Unreleased Artist is, so I'll say it directly.

I did this alone for a long time. The music, the creative vision, the doubt, the weight of every role. Alongside a full career and a family and everything else a full life asks of you. And I did it without the support I'm describing here because it didn't exist in the form I needed it.

There were coaches. There were mentors. There were communities. None of them quite held the specific intersection of things that made my situation real, the creative life alongside the professional life, the inner work alongside the practical plan, the music alongside the person making it.

So I became the person I needed. And then I built the place I wished had existed.

The Unreleased Artist is for the music creative who has been doing this alone. Who is carrying the vision, the doubt, the frustrations and the questions that haven't been answered yet. Who knows there's a better version of themselves and their work possible and who hasn't yet had the right support to get there.

You don't have to keep doing it alone. That's the whole point.

The loneliness is real. Naming it doesn't make it disappear. But it does make it something you can work with rather than something that works on you.

And the first step toward not doing this alone is saying, honestly, that you've been doing it alone. Which is harder than it sounds. And more important than almost anything else you could do today.
